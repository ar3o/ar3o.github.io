Editable 3D Scenes from Single Hyperspectral and RGB Surgical Frames

September 2026 · Hyperspectral imaging, monocular 3D reconstruction, NVIDIA Omniverse

Motivation

Surgical hyperspectral imaging measures tissue composition beyond what an RGB camera can see, which makes it useful for judging perfusion and telling tissues apart during an operation. The difficulty is data. Labelled acquisitions are few, and the variation that matters in practice, such as viewpoint, tissue motion, illumination, wet reflection and physiology, is rarely recorded under control.

The usual workarounds each miss something. Standard augmentation warps the image in 2D, so it cannot produce parallax or occlusion. Generative models need the training data that is missing in the first place. Fully simulated scenes give control, but they neither look nor measure like real tissue, so a model trained on them learns the simulator.

This work takes a different route. A single real frame is lifted into a 3D scene that keeps its measured appearance, and controlled variation is generated around it. Every rendered pixel is tied to a source pixel, so labels and correspondences are known by construction rather than estimated. The question is whether variation built this way is a better source of training and testing data than 2D augmentation.

Single-frame 3D reconstruction

The input is a calibrated reflectance cube, either a hyperspectral frame with 16 to 20 bands or an RGB frame. Before it can be lifted to 3D, the frame has to lose the traces of the light that recorded it, because anything left in the texture would move with the tissue in every new view.

Removing the original acquisition

SAM 3 is prompted with the names of instruments and other non-tissue material, and those pixels are excluded from the tissue surface. Specular highlights are then located with HFA-Net, a highlight detector adapted here to hyperspectral data by sharing one mask across all wavelengths. Its threshold, minimum region size and mask dilation were tuned on renders of cleaned tissue with and without a wet layer, where the true highlight positions are known. The masked regions are filled with LaMa, a neural inpainting model, run on each band separately. Finally, the illumination estimate of Marigold's intrinsic image decomposition is computed on a pseudo-RGB image and applied as one spatial gain to every wavelength, which flattens the scope's light falloff without changing the spectral shape of any pixel.

Geometry and correspondence

MoGe, a monocular geometry model, predicts a 3D point and a surface normal for every pixel. Neighbouring points are connected into a mesh of the visible surface, and each vertex stores the normalised image coordinate it came from. When a new view is rendered, the visible coordinate at each output pixel is used to sample the original reflectance cube and any label. Every spectrum therefore stays attached to the same surface point as the camera moves, and labels and correspondences are exact by construction. Pixels that reveal tissue hidden in the original frame are marked as invalid rather than filled with invented tissue.

Controlled variation

Once a frame is a surface, the factors that are entangled in real data can be varied one at a time while correspondences and labels remain exact. Each factor has bounded controls, and domain randomisation samples them with a low-discrepancy Sobol sequence so that new acquisitions cover the control space evenly instead of clustering around the original settings.

Viewpoint

A virtual endoscope can change its direction, distance, field of view, roll and target point. Camera positions are sampled on the upper hemisphere above the tissue and are kept above the tissue horizon, because a single view contains no surface below it.

Deformation

Tissue motion is modelled with smooth displacement fields applied to the mesh vertices. A vertex at position v moves to v′ = v + aL u(q, φ), where q is its normalised position, a the strength, L the surface size and φ a phase. Bending, twisting, folding and local displacement each use their own field u, for example a Gaussian bump for local displacement. Strength is expressed relative to the surface size because monocular geometry has no metric scale. The material coordinates never change, so every label and spectrum follows the same tissue through the deformation.

Illumination and reflection

New acquisitions are rendered with Mitsuba 3, a spectral light-transport renderer. The cleaned reflectance sits beneath a rough dielectric interface that approximates the wet tissue boundary, with its roughness, specular strength and refractive index varied. A variable number of light sources is placed around the camera and aimed at the tissue, like the light ports of an endoscope, and their position, intensity, beam spread and spectral power distribution are sampled. Rendered radiance is divided by the response of a synthetic white reference, so every new view is again a reflectance image.

Physiology

Viewpoint and lighting do not change the tissue itself. To change its physiology, the measured spectra are fitted to a three-layer optical tissue model with 18 parameters, including oxygen saturation shared across layers and the blood volume, scattering, anisotropy, refractive index and thickness of each layer. The forward model is MCMLNet, a neural surrogate of multilayer Monte Carlo light transport, combined with the camera's band responses.

Fitting every pixel would be slow and unstable, so the tissue pixels are grouped into 128 clusters by spectral similarity and position, and the median spectrum of each cluster is fitted. For each cluster, 32,768 tissue states drawn from a Sobol design are screened, and the four best are refined by gradient descent on a loss that combines relative reflectance error with the spectral angle. Fits are kept only within a spectral angle of 5° and a normalised error of 10%, and every solution close to the best is kept, because different tissue states can produce the same spectrum. An edit, such as lower oxygen saturation inside a chosen region, changes the fitted state, and the ratio between the edited and original model spectra is applied to the measured spectrum. The measured texture is therefore changed only by the modelled physiological difference.

Light transport in curved tissue

The three-layer model assumes a flat slab lit from above, while real tissue is curved and often lit at a grazing angle. To measure what this assumption misses, photon transport was simulated with mesh-based Monte Carlo in tetrahedral meshes of curved surfaces, including cylinders, spheres and folds, at illumination angles from 30° to 89°. Each curved case was paired with a flat, normally lit reference of the same composition, across 36 wavelengths from 500 to 760 nm. A small network with 304 parameters then learns the change in spectral shape from the affected spectrum and its illumination angle, which can be computed from the surface normal, so that curved views can be corrected towards what the flat model expects.

Scene editing in Omniverse

The scenes are exported as USD and open in an NVIDIA Omniverse extension, where a real acquisition can be inspected from any angle and path traced. Scope lighting, surgical smoke and instrument models can be added, and an instrument can grasp and lift the tissue with finite-element deformation. Because the underlying scene is real, these edits give controlled stress tests for trained models on real anatomy rather than on a synthetic stand-in, and the same scenes can feed Replicator for domain randomisation or Isaac for robot learning.

Data

The work uses porcine open-surgery hyperspectral acquisitions (HeiPorSPECTRAL and Xeno-learning), human minimally invasive hyperspectral data from EnAcuity, the HyKey hyperspectral matching data, and RGB laparoscopy from DSAD, StereoMIS, STIR and SERV-CT.
